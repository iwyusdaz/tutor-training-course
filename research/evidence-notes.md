## 1. The scale problem: effects shrink when tutoring scales

### 1a. Small-scale trials showed large effects

**Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan (2020)** — meta-analysis of pre-pandemic randomized controlled trials of tutoring, pooled effect **0.37 SD**. Translated by Robinson et al. (2021) into **3–15 months of additional learning**.

- Verified via CALDER Working Paper 338-0426 (§2, Literature Review), which states: *"A well-cited meta-analysis of pre-pandemic randomized control trials, for example, found a pooled effect size of 0.37 standard deviation units (Nickow et al., 2020), translating into three to 15 months of additional learning (Robinson et al., 2021)."*
- URL (accessed 2026-10-25): https://caldercenter.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/CALDER-WP-338-0426.pdf

### 1b. At scale, the effect is roughly a third to a half as large

**Kraft, Schueler & Falken (2024), "What Impacts Should We Expect from Tutoring at Scale? Exploring Meta-Analytic Generalizability"** (EdWorking Paper 24-1031; later published in *Review of Educational Research*). Meta-analysis of **282 RCTs**. I read the full working-paper PDF.

- In the preferred analytic samples (large-scale U.S. programs measured on standardized tests): pooled effect **0.21 SD for programs serving 400–999 students** and **0.16 SD for programs serving 1,000+ students** — *"only a third to a half as large as those from our full sample"*, with the decline *"driven by stark declines in pooled effect sizes as tutoring program scale increases."*
- **Virtual tutoring: pooled 0.07 SD** (59 estimates, 6 studies), vs 0.44 SD unadjusted pooled for in-person programs.
- Direct quote: *"Still, such effects for tutoring at scale are far from guaranteed."*
- URL (accessed 2026-10-25): https://edworkingpapers.com/ai24-1031 · PDF: https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai24-1031.pdf
- **Correction of the portfolio plan:** the plan sketched at-scale effects as "around 0.03–0.10 SD". The primary source does not support that range: it reports 0.16–0.21 SD for large in-person programs and 0.07 SD for virtual delivery. This repo therefore says "roughly a third to a half as large as small-scale trials (about 0.16–0.21 SD for large programs in preferred samples, ~0.07 SD for virtual)", with the citation above. The direction of the claim (large ≠ guaranteed; implementation quality is what erodes at scale) is unchanged.

### 1c. A district case study: quality infrastructure is how scale is survived

**Hashim, Postell, Leak & Hamilton (April 2026), "The End of ESSER: Lessons about Sustaining Tutoring at Scale from Guilford County Schools"** (CALDER Working Paper 338-0426). I read the full PDF. Key verified findings:

- Guilford County Schools built a home-grown high-dosage tutoring program; ESSER-era barriers were staffing, scheduling, and fidelity; the district responded with **system-level supports**: a personnel infrastructure for tutor hiring/quality, increased tutor supervision for data use and curriculum planning, and MTSS/data dashboards to enforce high-dosage practices (small groups, session frequency, duration, pull-out delivery).
- **Dosage monitoring in practice:** average sessions per student rose from 26.21 → 28.04 → 30.09 across three years, *"gains that approach the program target of at least 27 sessions annually"*; district leaders *"used real-time data systems to monitor tutor attendance and dosage."*
- *"Improvements in these areas were easier to consolidate as program scale decreased post-ESSER"* — quality infrastructure is easiest to hold at right-sized scale.
- Persistent challenge areas: middle school math, hard-to-staff schools.
- URL (accessed 2026-10-25): https://caldercenter.org/sites/default/files/2026-04/CALDER-WP-338-0426.pdf

**→ Design implications (1a–1c):** training cannot be a slide deck on day one. The program must (a) define observable tutor behaviors so they can be coached and observed, (b) treat fidelity data (dosage, consistency, observed quality) as leading indicators, and (c) give coordinators lightweight observation tools. This drove the competency model, the observation rubric, and the measurement plan.

## 2. The dosage benchmark: ~90 minutes/week, and what students actually got

**Pearl Education & Whiteboard Advisors (2026-10-07), "The State of Tutoring 2026: What Leaders Can See and What They Can't"** — press release and report page read directly (the analysis of ~884,000 sessions; the largest cross-state implementation dataset cited in the plan's news coverage).

- **Benchmark:** *"Research on effective high-dosage tutoring sets a standard of roughly 90 minutes of tutoring a week, delivered in three small group 30-minute sessions, and the typical student in the analysis received about 72 minutes a week."*
- Dataset: **883,882 sessions delivered to 64,044 students across 28 programs** (state-, district- and vendor-led), 2025–26 school year.
- **The "visibility gap":** *"91% of the difference in weekly minutes was among students in the same program, rather than between programs."* In one district of 6,000+ students, weekly minutes ranged from 23 to 96 per student. Program averages hide individual under-dosage.
- Other verified figures: median duration 12 weeks; nearly 1 in 4 students had fewer than 8 weeks; only 10% received 3+ sessions/week sustained for 12+ weeks; 83% of scheduled sessions attended (89% in 1:1); tutor continuity 84% of students in district programs had the same tutor all year vs ~4 in 10 in state-led and vendor-led programs.
- URLs (accessed 2026-10-25): press release https://poweredbypearl.com/tutoring-nears-research-benchmark-after-esser-but-averages-hide-big-differences-among-students-analysis-of-nearly-900000-sessions-finds/ · report page https://poweredbypearl.com/the-state-of-tutoring-2026/
- Limitation stated by the report itself: single platform, not nationally representative.

**→ Design implications:** the fictional program sets **90 min/week (3 × 30 min) as its dosage target and flags any student below 60 min/week**, tracks **per-student** dosage (not program averages), and treats **tutor consistency (≥85% same-tutor sessions)** as a tracked metric. The simulated dashboard's under-delivering school exists because within-program variation is exactly where programs fail.

## 3. What makes tutoring "high-impact": the NSSA definition

**National Student Support Accelerator (Stanford), "High-impact tutoring" definition page** — read directly.

- Quoted definition: *"High-impact tutoring is intensive, relationship-based, and individualized instruction that leads to substantial learning gains for students."*
- Verified design characteristics: **3+ sessions per week; 10+ weeks; 1:1 optimal, no larger than 1:4; consistent, well-trained tutors; during the school day; vetted, structured materials aligned to core instruction; data-driven instruction with close monitoring of student skills.** Programs with these elements yield 3–15 months of additional learning.
- URL (accessed 2026-10-25): https://nssa.stanford.edu/about/high-impact-tutoring

**→ Design implications:** the fictional program (Bridge Math Tutoring) is defined inside these characteristics: in-school-day sessions, groups of 2–4, same tutor, 3 × 30 min/week, structured session routine, exit-ticket data loop. The competency model's "Session structure" and "Data use" domains exist because the definition names them as design features, not accidents.

## 4. Post-ESSER reality: a sorting point, not a cliff

**Lu, Hashim, Gilbert, Zhou & Loeb (2026-09-15), "How is high-impact tutoring enduring after ESSER?" (NSSA brief)** — read directly.

- Survey of **35 districts**: after ESSER, programs sorted onto four paths — **paused or disrupted 23% (8/35); significantly reduced scale 23% (8/35); maintained scale 31% (11/35); increased scale 23% (8/35)**. Nationally, School Pulse Panel data showed tutoring activity *"decreased slightly"* as ESSER closed.
- Context: ESSER was a $190B federal investment; CCSSO estimated **$4.4 billion** had been spent on tutoring and learning acceleration as of March 2024.
- Four sustainability strategies named in the brief: (1) a "district champion" advocating with data narratives; (2) **"right-sizing"** — serving fewer students more consistently to raise fidelity; (3) integrating tutoring into MTSS structures; (4) local tutor pipelines and community partnerships to cut cost (one district ran an in-house program for ~$150K/year).
- The brief defines high-impact tutoring qualitatively (citing Robinson & Loeb 2021) and reports **no effect sizes** — it is an implementation brief, not an outcomes study.
- URL (accessed 2026-10-25): https://nssa.stanford.edu/briefs/how-is-high-impact-tutoring-enduring-after-esser

**NSSA, "How districts can fund high-quality tutoring now that ESSER money is gone" (news page)** — read directly.

- Tutoring tends to produce substantial gains when *"delivered during the school day, in small groups with the same tutors and multiple times a week for at least 10 weeks."*
- Cost range: **$1,200–$2,500 per student per year** depending on model and staffing.
- URL (accessed 2026-10-25): https://nssa.stanford.edu/news/how-districts-can-fund-high-quality-tutoring-now-esser-money-gone

**→ Design implications:** program managers under budget pressure will cut training and coaching first, because its value is invisible. The measurement plan therefore makes **fidelity and tutor development visible metrics** (observation rubric growth, exit-ticket completion) — the things a "district champion" can put in a data narrative to defend the program. The fictional program's constraint ("quality with tighter budgets") matches the brief's right-sizing reality.

## 5. The tutoring moves: what to train and observe

The canonical move names and hint-ladder levels used throughout this repo are **shared with the companion repo [Socratic AI Tutor](https://iwyusdaz.github.io/socratic-ai-tutor/)** and come from its `spec/pedagogy-rules.md` v1.0 (2026-10-25), which grounds each move in learning research (Chi & Wylie 2014; Hattie & Timperley 2007; Aleven et al. 2003; VanLehn 2011; Kapur 2008; Sweller et al. 2019; Knuth et al. 2006):

1. **probing question**
2. **hint ladder (levels 0–5)** — L0 probing question ("What have you tried?") → L1 conceptual nudge → L2 strategy hint → L3 partial step → L4 worked example of a similar problem → L5 full solution + new problem (last resort)
3. **wait time**
4. **specific feedback**
5. **worked example after repeated failure**
6. **confirm and ask why**
7. **offer a similar problem**

The five linear-equation misconceptions the transcripts and exit tickets target (same taxonomy as the companion repo): **M1** applying an operation to only one side · **M2** sign errors when moving terms · **M3** distributing incorrectly (`2(x − 4) → 2x − 4`) · **M4** dividing only some terms · **M5** equals sign as "the answer comes next", not as balance (M5 grounded in Knuth, Stephens, McNeil & Alibali 2006).

**→ Design implications:** modules, transcripts, the spot-the-move tool, the rubric and the fidelity metrics all use these exact names — one vocabulary across training, observation and measurement, and across both repos ("the same moves, encoded for an AI"). Where a move was designed for chat, the human adaptation is stated (e.g., wait time as a silent 3–5 second pause; the hint ladder spoken one rung per turn).
