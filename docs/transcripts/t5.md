# Transcript T5 · The tutor explains everything

{: .label .label-red }
Weak session · Tutor: Nadia (new tutor, week 2) · Students: Imani, Ravi, Cole (Grade 7) · Planned skill: solving `a(x + b) = c` (distribute first); misconception in play: distributing incorrectly (M3)

{: .note }
**Fictional transcript, constructed for training purposes.** Invented tutor and students; not a recording of real children. Numbered annotations follow each exchange — [format](index.md). Nadia is written as sympathetic on purpose: the over-explaining failure mode is what conscientious beginners drift into when they prepare well and trust explaining.

---

**[0:00]**

**Nadia:** Hi Imani, Ravi, Cole! I'm so glad you're here. I made us a whole plan for today — I stayed up making it really clear, I promise. So today we're doing equations with parentheses, which look scary but are actually the *easiest* kind once you see the trick, because all you do is distribute first, and distributing just means multiplying the outside number by everything inside, like 2 times (x + 3) becomes 2x + 6, but you have to remember the sign, so if it's (x − 3) then it's 2x − 6, okay?

> **[1] Q1 (violation: zero questions in 90 seconds — the session opens with a solution monologue).** Everything Nadia says is mathematically true. It is also the *answer to a question nobody asked*: no student has attempted, erred, or wondered yet. The [hint ladder](https://github.com/iwyusdaz/socratic-ai-tutor/blob/main/spec/hint-ladder.md) starts at L0 ("What have you tried?") for a reason: the attempt is the diagnosis. Also note R2 — no reference to any recorded work; "I made us a whole plan" centers the tutor's effort, not the students'.

**Imani:** …okay.

**Nadia:** So example one, let me just show you. `3(x + 2) = 12`. First I distribute: 3 × x is 3x, and 3 × 2 is 6, so `3x + 6 = 12`. Then I subtract 6 from both sides — and "both sides" is important, I'll say why in a second — so `3x = 6`. Then divide both sides by 3, and that gives me x = 2. See? Easy!

> **[2] Q1 again, compounded (the tutor performs the *entire* solution; the students produce nothing).** Count the verbs: *I distribute, I subtract, that gives me*. In the strong transcripts ([T1](t1.md), [T2](t2.md)) the students hold the pen and the verbs. The rubric's Domain 3 anchor for level 1 is nearly this line: "explains the method without asking for an attempt."

**Cole:** I think I got it.

**Nadia:** You will! Example two, watch: `4(x − 1) = 8`. So we distribute — 4x minus 4 — then add 4 to both sides, `4x = 12`, divide, x = 3. The trick is always distribute first, always balance both sides. Same thing, just different numbers!

> **[3] R4 + Q4 (nobody has been asked anything; Cole's "I think I got it" — an offer to attempt! — is waved past instead of taken up).** "You will!" is warm. It also converts Cole from a prospective thinker into an audience member. The take-up move: "Show me on your paper, Cole — I'll watch."

**[0:09] "Guided practice"**

**Nadia:** Now you try — well, we'll do it together, I'll guide. `2(x − 4) = 2x − 4`, is that right, Imani?

**Imani:** I think so…?

**Nadia:** You're close, but no — when you distribute, the 2 has to multiply *both* things inside, so it's 2x − 8, not 2x − 4. Remember the sign gets multiplied too. Let me write it: 2 times −4 is −8. Okay?

> **[4] Q3 (violation: the M3 error — distributing incorrectly — is *named and corrected outright*, the exact opposite of the expose-don't-correct rule).** Worse: Imani was never asked to say what she meant, so we don't know *why* she wrote it (a guess? a real misconception? a typo?). And "you're close, but no" — to an *is-it-right* question Nadia herself framed — models answer-checking as approval-seeking from the tutor. The strong version ([T2](t2.md) annotation 4): "Hold on — walk me through what happened to the −4."

**Ravi:** So we just always multiply the outside number by both?

**Nadia:** Yes, exactly! You've got it!

> **[5] F1 (violation: "You've got it!" for *restating the rule Nadia just said twice* — specific feedback can't name a process Ravi never ran).** Note Ravi's question is a good one — but the confirmation he receives certifies a sentence, not a skill.

**[0:17] — the clock is the tutor now**

**Nadia:** Time check — okay, I planned four practice problems, we'll do them together really fast. `5(x + 1) = 15`… distribute: 5x + 5 = 15… subtract 5: 5x = 10… divide: x = 2. Next one, `3(x + 4) = 21` — 3x + 12 = 21, 3x = 9, x = 3. You try the last one with me: `6(x − 2) = 12`. First step? …distribute, right? 6x − 12 = 12. Then?

**Cole:** Plus 12?

**Nadia:** Plus 12 both sides! 6x = 24, so x = 4. Perfect, you did it!

> **[6] S1 (the guided-practice box has become a solo lecture with call-and-response — "you did it" certifies one word, "plus," from a student repeating a pattern Nadia is feeding him).** The "together really fast" session is the over-explainer's signature: content coverage *replaces* student production, and pace is the proof everything's fine.

**[0:27]**

**Nadia:** Oh no, we're out of time — the exit ticket! We'll do it Thursday, I promise, we just ran over because the examples took longer than I thought. Same time Thursday — you guys were *great* today.

> **[7] S3/D1 (violation: the exit ticket is skipped — the fourth time this week for this group; the [measurement plan](../program/measurement-plan.md) will show this group's sessions-with-ticket rate at 40% and the data gap will be invisible in the dashboard).** And see the causal chain: over-explaining [1–3] consumed the minutes; the minutes were *protected by nothing* (no timer discipline [6]); the data loop died last. Structure is what defends the student's thinking *and* the program's data.

## Why this transcript is here

Nadia is the most common week-2 tutor: prepared, warm, mathematically correct — and doing all the work. The session's failures are all one failure: **the students never thought.**

| What Nadia did | What the rubric asks for | Where it's trained |
|---|---|---|
| Opened with a 90-second monologue [1] | L0 probing question before any explaining | [Module 3](../modules/3-questions-wait-time-and-errors.md) |
| Performed every solution herself [2][3] | Student holds the pen; tutor asks "what's your first step?" | [Module 3](../modules/3-questions-wait-time-and-errors.md) |
| Corrected the M3 error outright [4] | Expose, don't correct: "What did the 2 multiply?" | [Module 3](../modules/3-questions-wait-time-and-errors.md), misconception M3 |
| Certified a restated rule [5] | Specific feedback names *what the student's own work* did right | [Module 4](../modules/4-feedback-and-motivation.md) |
| Skipped the exit ticket [7] | Ticket every session, collected even if incomplete | [Module 2](../modules/2-the-30-minute-session.md) |
| No references to recorded work [1] | R1–R3 greeting + reference routine | [Module 1](../modules/1-your-role-and-relationships.md) |

## Rubric read

[Observation rubric](../coaching/observation-rubric.md): Domain 1 = 2 (warm, names used; no work references) · Domain 2 = 1 (blocks present as a slide deck, not a routine; no ticket) · Domain 3 = 1 (no probing questions; the one error surfaced is corrected outright) · Domain 4 = 2 (enthusiastic generic praise) · Domain 5 = 1 (no ticket, no data). T5 is the calibration anchor for **Domain 3, level 1** — and for the coaching move of leading with the *controllable* fix: one probing question per problem, before anything else changes.
