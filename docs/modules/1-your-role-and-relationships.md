# Module 1 · Your role and relationships

{: .label .label-green }
About 20 minutes · Competency domain: **Relationships** · [competency model](/tutor-training-course/research/competency-model.md)

## Objective

By the end of this module you can: **run the first three minutes of a session so that every student is greeted by name and hears one specific reference to their previous work** — behaviors R1–R2 in the [competency model](/tutor-training-course/research/competency-model.md), scored in [rubric Domain 1](/tutor-training-course/coaching/observation-rubric/).

## Scenario hook: Diego's first session

> Diego, 20, is a sophomore tutoring his first group of three Grade 7 students. He prepared hard. He walks in, sits down, opens the worksheet and says: "Okay! So today we're doing two-step equations. Let me show you how these work — first you subtract the constant, then you divide."
>
> Two students follow. One, Amara, puts her head down. Diego talks *at* the group for 25 minutes, answers his own questions, and never learns that Amara failed the same quiz step twice last week — because nobody told him to look.

Nothing Diego said about math was wrong. The session still failed, because a tutor's first job in the room is **relational, not informational**. The [National Student Support Accelerator's](https://nssa.stanford.edu/about/high-impact-tutoring) definition of high-impact tutoring starts with "relationship-based" — and that is a trainable behavior, not a personality trait.

## Key idea: four behaviors students can feel

The competency model defines the Relationships domain by what an observer can *see*:

1. **Greet every student by name** in the first minute (R1).
2. **Reference the previous session** at least once — their exit ticket, a problem they cracked, a mistake you already worked through (R2).
3. **Spread attention across the group** — every student speaks or works with you at least once; no one is carried, no one is left (R4).
4. **Keep and use progress notes** — names of effort, not just scores (R3).

Why it matters: tutoring works through a consistent adult who knows the student's work. The [State of Tutoring 2026 analysis](https://poweredbypearl.com/tutoring-nears-research-benchmark-after-esser-but-averages-hide-big-differences-among-students-analysis-of-nearly-900000-sessions-finds/) found students in district programs kept the same tutor all year **84%** of the time — versus about 4 in 10 in state-led and vendor-led programs. Consistency is the asset; the relationship is how you compound it.

## Model: the first three minutes

This excerpt is from [Transcript T1](/tutor-training-course/transcripts/t1/), a **fictional, constructed-for-training** session with three students.

> **Tutor:** Welcome back, Amara, Jayden, Luis — grab the same seats. Amara, yesterday your distributive step finally clicked; today we start there. [**R1 + R2**]
>
> **Amara:** The one with the parentheses?
>
> **Tutor:** That's the one. Luis, last week you said negative numbers were "out to get you" — your exit ticket proved otherwise, so I'm watching you today. [**R2, specific**]
>
> **Luis:** Ha! Okay.
>
> **Tutor:** Warm-up's on the board, three quick problems, alone start. [**R4: everyone has a job within 60 seconds**]

**What to notice:** the references are *specific and public* but never embarrassing — Amara gets a win named, not a failure. Every student is individually addressed inside 60 seconds.

## Practice with feedback

**Your turn (3 minutes):** You inherit a group you have met twice. Amara's last two exit tickets: solved `3x + 5 = 20` fine, then wrote `2(x − 4) = 2x − 4` (distributed to the first term only). Jayden aced both. Luis missed the sign when moving terms: wrote `x − 4 = 10 → x = 6`.

Write your **first three sentences** to the group. Then compare:

{: .feedback }
**Strong example:** "Welcome back. Amara, yesterday you fixed the parentheses problem yourself — that's where we start today. Jayden, I've got a challenge problem with your name on it. Luis, before we begin: walk me through what happened with that minus 4, because I think it's easier than it looks."
**Why it works:** every student is named (R1); two references tie to recorded work (R2); the "challenge problem" pre-empts Jayden dominating (R4); Luis's error is framed as tractable, not as a deficit.
**Weak version:** "Hi everyone, ready for math?" — no names, no history, no plan. It costs nothing and buys nothing.

## In your next session, try…

1. Write each student's **name + one true sentence from their last exit ticket** on your session plan before you walk in ([session plan template](/tutor-training-course/tools/session-plan-template/)).
2. Say both out loud in the first three minutes.
3. Mark an **attention tick** next to each name when they speak — at session end, no name without a tick.

## Reflection

After your next session, answer in the [tutor self-reflection form](/tutor-training-course/tools/tutor-self-reflection/): *Which student did I address least, and what did I miss as a result?* Bring the answer to your next coaching conversation — it is a standard opener in the [coaching conversation guide](/tutor-training-course/coaching/coaching-conversation-guide/).

## Check yourself

- A student says "I'm just bad at math" in week 1. Which of the four behaviors is your best first response? *(Reference their previous work — R2. Concrete past evidence contradicts the identity claim better than reassurance does.)*
- Your strongest student finishes early and starts answering for the others. Which behavior are you protecting? *(R4 — spread attention. Redirect: "Amara, what do you think of Jayden's first step?")*
