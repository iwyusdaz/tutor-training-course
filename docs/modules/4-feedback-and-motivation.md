# Module 4 · Feedback and motivation

{: .label .label-green }
About 20 minutes · Competency domain: **Feedback and motivation** · [competency model](https://github.com/iwyusdaz/tutor-training-course/blob/main/research/competency-model.md)

## Objectives

By the end of this module you can:
1. **Give specific, process-focused feedback** — name *what in the student's work* was right, not a verdict on the student (behaviors F1–F2, [rubric Domain 4](../coaching/observation-rubric.md)).
2. **After a correct answer: confirm briefly, ask one why-question, then offer a similar problem** (F3–F4) — the exact chain the [Socratic AI Tutor](https://iwyusdaz.github.io/socratic-ai-tutor/) encodes as its "confirm and ask why" and "offer a similar problem" moves.

## Scenario hook: twenty "good job"s

> Priya's coordinator observes her session and tallies her praise: "good job" ×14, "nice" ×5, "perfect" ×1. The praise is sincere, warm — and nearly useless. Two students who solved correctly can't say *what* they did right. One who earned real praise for a repaired sign error hears the same "good job" as the student who copied a neighbor.
>
> Feedback research says process-level, specific feedback outperforms person-level praise — the same evidence base the [Socratic AI Tutor's rules](https://github.com/iwyusdaz/socratic-ai-tutor/blob/main/spec/pedagogy-rules.md) cite (Hattie & Timperley 2007). In a 30-minute small group, that feedback also *is* the relationship: it's how students learn that you actually see their work.

## Key idea: three habits

**1. Specific feedback names the work.** "You moved the −4 to the other side *and* changed its sign — that's the step you missed last week" tells the student what to keep doing. "Great job" tells the student nothing except that an adult is pleased. A practical bar: your praise should contain one detail that couldn't be said to any other student.

**2. Confirm, then ask why — once.** When a student is right: confirm clearly ("That's right — x = 5"), ask one why-question ("Why did we subtract before dividing?"), let them answer, then move on. The why is where success turns into understanding; endless questioning after a right answer teaches students that being correct buys more interrogation, which teaches them to hide correctness.

**3. Offer a similar problem — don't assign it.** After a solved problem: "New one, same shape: `4x − 3 = 17`. Want to try it — I'll say less this time?" This is the cheapest honest test that the fix transferred, and the offer (not the assignment) keeps ownership with the student.

**Normalize errors as you go:** "That's the trap this problem sets — let's look at it" beats "no, again?" every time. Students who believe errors are information take the risks that produce thinking.

## Model: a solved problem, closed properly

Verbatim excerpt from [Transcript T1](../transcripts/t1.md) (**fictional, constructed for training**) — the end of guided practice: confirm, ask why once, then offer the similar problem:

> **Luis:** …plus 5 both sides? So `2x = 16`.
>
> **Nina:** Say why plus.
>
> **Luis:** To move the −5… and the sign flips because we're subtracting minus 5.
>
> **Nina:** You kept both sides balanced — that's the move. Now x? [confirm and ask why]
>
> **Luis:** Divide by 2 — `x = 8`!
>
> **Nina:** Check it for us.
>
> **Luis:** 2(8 − 4) + 3… 8 + 3 = 11. Yes! [the student checks his own work]
>
> **Nina:** Same shape, new numbers: `3(x + 2) − 5 = 16`. Solo, four minutes — I'm not talking. If you finish, check by substituting. [offer a similar problem]

**What to notice:** the confirmation names the *process* ("you kept both sides balanced"), the why comes once and then the tutor moves on, and the similar problem arrives as an offer with the tutor going silent — ownership stays with the student. (For the specific-feedback habit — naming the work, not the person — see T1's opening exchange and annotation 6.)

## Practice with feedback

**Your turn — upgrade these three.** Rewrite each as specific feedback (one detail, process-focused):

1. "Good job!" — after Amara correctly distributes in `3(x + 2) − 4`.
2. "You're so smart." — after Luis solves `4x = 12` without hints.
3. "Perfect!" — after Mei fixes her own sign error on the second try.

{: .feedback }
**Strong rewrites:**
1. "You distributed to *both* terms — the `+2` didn't escape this time."
2. "You divided every term by 4 in one move — no hints from me on that one."
3. "You checked your signs before I said anything — that's exactly the habit from last session."
**Why:** each names the *behavior* that produced the success, so the student knows what to repeat. "Smart" teaches students to protect an identity instead of practicing a process — and it collapses the first time they struggle.

## In your next session, try…

1. Give every student **one** piece of specific feedback tied to their recorded work ([tracker](../tools/student-progress-tracker.md)).
2. After each correct answer: confirm → one why → offer a similar problem. Three chains in one session is a good session.
3. Catch one error early and say "that's the trap this problem sets" — out loud, warmly, before anyone else notices.

## Reflection

In the [tutor self-reflection form](../tools/tutor-self-reflection.md): *Whose success did I praise most this week? What did I actually name? Which student never heard their own work described?*

## Check yourself

- A student solves fast and asks for harder work. Confirm-and-why, similar problem — or jump to new content? *(Offer a similar problem first: one small variation tests whether the method — not just this answer — transferred. Then escalate.)*
- A student is visibly deflated after a third error. What sequence? *(Normalize briefly, then the worked example after repeated failure (L4): work a similar problem fully, then hand back the original. Comfort without a plan just postpones the frustration.)*
